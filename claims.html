Claim vs Evidence

Each entry below takes a specific public claim and sets it directly against the exact wording of the source documents. The evidence status reflects what the documents actually say — no more, and no less — including where the record is still incomplete. More entries are added as they are researched and checked.

"The children's remains were dumped in a septic tank."

Where this claim has appeared
Widely repeated in national and international media coverage since 2017, and in general public discussion of the Tuam site.
Relevant source documents
Commission of Investigation statement, 3 March 2017 (Document Library, TRP-002); ODAIT forensic updates, 2025–2026 (TRP-010, TRP-011).
Exact source wording
"Test trenches were dug revealing two large structures. One structure appears to be a large sewage containment system or septic tank that had been decommissioned and filled with rubble and debris and then covered with top soil. The second structure is a long structure which is divided into 20 chambers. The Commission has not yet determined what the purpose of this structure was but it appears to be related to the treatment/containment of sewage and/or waste water... In this second structure, significant quantities of human remains have been discovered in at least 17 of the 20 underground chambers which were examined."

— Commission of Investigation, 3 March 2017

Plain-English explanation
The Commission's own 2017 statement describes two separate structures. The first — the one it explicitly calls a decommissioned "sewage containment system or septic tank" — was filled with rubble and covered over; no remains were reported found in it. The remains were found in the second structure, a 20-chambered underground feature whose original purpose the Commission said it had "not yet determined," beyond noting it appeared sewage- or wastewater-related. In short: the popular shorthand "septic tank" conflates the two structures. The precise nature of the chamber where remains were actually found — and how the remains came to be there — is something the Commission itself said was not yet established in 2017, and it remains a subject of the ongoing forensic excavation.
Evidence status
Partially supported, imprecisely stated — human remains were confirmed in an underground chamber structure, which is documented fact. Whether "septic tank" is an accurate description of that specific structure, and the manner and intent behind how remains came to be there, is not settled by the 2017 statement and is an area the ongoing 2025–2027 excavation is expected to clarify further.
Unresolved questions
What was the chamber structure's original function, and was it in active use during the period remains were interred? Who was responsible for placing remains there, and under what circumstances? These are explicitly still open, per ODAIT's own public updates.

"The Bon Secours Sisters deliberately buried children secretly in a disused sewage tank to hide the deaths."

Where this claim has appeared
Widely repeated in media and public commentary since 2017, often stated as settled fact.
Relevant source documents
Fifth Interim Report of the Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation, 15 March 2019 (Document Library, TRP-004); Irish Times report on the same, 5 June 2019.
Exact source wording
"[The structure] was not a recognised burial ground or purpose-built burial chamber"… it "did not provide for the dignified interment of human remains" and was "likely" related to the treatment of sewage and/or waste water. "If it had been designed as a crypt, an entirely different type of structure would have been expected." The Commission also found it "likely the burials were conducted on the instructions of the sisters."

— Fifth Interim Report, Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation, March 2019, as reported by the Irish Times

The other side of this specific finding
The Commission shared its draft technical findings with the Bon Secours congregation before publishing. The congregation commissioned its own expert review, which "suggested that the [Commission's] archaeological reports did not adequately consider the possibility that the structure in which the human remains were found was designed as a burial vault and then used as such" — in other words, the Sisters' own expert disputes the Commission's reading of the physical evidence. It is also on record that Galway County Council — which owned the home and had the legal duty to keep a burial register — did not respond when the Commission shared its draft and technical reports with it in 2017 and 2018.
Plain-English explanation
The Commission's official finding is that the structure was not a proper burial ground and did not provide dignified interment, and that burial there was likely done on the Sisters' instructions. That is the official record. But "deliberately hidden to conceal deaths" is a claim about intent that goes beyond what the Commission's finding states directly, and it is contested by the Sisters' own expert on the physical evidence itself. Separately, no burial register was ever kept by Galway County Council either, despite it being under a legal obligation to keep one — a State and local-authority failure that runs alongside the Sisters' role, not instead of it.
Evidence status
Officially found, but part of it is contested — that burial there was inappropriate and not dignified is an official finding. That it amounted to a deliberate cover-up, specifically to hide deaths, is an interpretation of that finding, not the finding's own wording, and the underlying archaeological reading itself is disputed by an expert engaged by the Bon Secours Sisters.
Unresolved questions
What specific instructions were given, by whom, and why. Why Galway County Council kept no burial register despite the legal duty to do so. Whether the disputed "burial vault" interpretation holds up against the ongoing forensic excavation evidence.

"The extremely high number of child deaths at Tuam shows deliberate cruelty or neglect unique to this home, or to the Bon Secours Sisters."

Where this claim has appeared
Common framing in media coverage and public commentary since 2017.
Relevant source documents
Final Report of the Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation (TRP-001); peer-reviewed study, "Infant Mortality in Mother and Baby Homes in 20th Century Ireland," Population Research and Policy Review, 2024.
What the figures show
Around 3,251 children were born at or admitted to the Tuam institution between 1921 and 1961; the Commission's Final Report records 978 child deaths over that period (roughly 29%). In 1933, the death rate reached 38.95%, about six times the national rate of 6.5% that year, and stayed above 30% intermittently until 1949 before falling. Ireland's national infant mortality rate itself was high by later standards throughout this period — 70 per 1,000 live births in 1930, 65 per 1,000 in 1940.
The 2024 peer-reviewed study, examining several mother and baby homes together, found excess mortality reaching roughly four times the national rate in the 1930s and 1940s across the homes studied — not only at Tuam — and that mortality rates in the homes "mainly resembled mortality rates among all infants of unmarried mothers" more broadly in Irish society at the time. The same study found a similar pattern of very high peak mortality (300–400 deaths per 1,000) at other homes run by different religious orders.
Plain-English explanation
Child mortality at Tuam was severely elevated compared with the general national rate — that part is well documented and not in dispute. But independent academic research comparing several mother and baby homes finds a broadly similar pattern of excess mortality across homes run by different orders, and links a meaningful part of that excess to the wider, well-documented risks facing children of unmarried mothers in Irish society generally during this period — not only to conditions inside any one institution. That context does not reduce the scale of loss, but the comparative evidence does not support singling out Tuam, or the Bon Secours Sisters specifically, as uniquely responsible for a pattern that appears across the wider system.
Evidence status
Documented pattern, not shown to be unique to Tuam or to this order
Unresolved questions
How much of Tuam's specific excess mortality is attributable to conditions inside the home itself (staffing, nutrition, medical care) versus these wider societal factors is a detailed question the Commission's Final Report goes into at length, and deserves its own fuller write-up as we add more of that report to the Document Library.
