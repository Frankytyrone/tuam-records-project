Claim vs Evidence

Each entry below takes a specific public claim and sets it directly against the exact wording of the source documents. The evidence status reflects what the documents actually say — no more, and no less — including where the record is still incomplete. More entries are added as they are researched and checked.

"The children's remains were dumped in a septic tank."

Where this claim has appeared
Widely repeated in national and international media coverage since 2017, and in general public discussion of the Tuam site.
Relevant source documents
Commission of Investigation statement, 3 March 2017 (Document Library, TRP-002); ODAIT forensic updates, 2025–2026 (TRP-010, TRP-011).
Exact source wording
"Test trenches were dug revealing two large structures. One structure appears to be a large sewage containment system or septic tank that had been decommissioned and filled with rubble and debris and then covered with top soil. The second structure is a long structure which is divided into 20 chambers. The Commission has not yet determined what the purpose of this structure was but it appears to be related to the treatment/containment of sewage and/or waste water... In this second structure, significant quantities of human remains have been discovered in at least 17 of the 20 underground chambers which were examined."

— Commission of Investigation, 3 March 2017

Plain-English explanation
The Commission's own 2017 statement describes two separate structures. The first — the one it explicitly calls a decommissioned "sewage containment system or septic tank" — was filled with rubble and covered over; no remains were reported found in it. The remains were found in the second structure, a 20-chambered underground feature whose original purpose the Commission said it had "not yet determined," beyond noting it appeared sewage- or wastewater-related. In short: the popular shorthand "septic tank" conflates the two structures. The precise nature of the chamber where remains were actually found — and how the remains came to be there — is something the Commission itself said was not yet established in 2017, and it remains a subject of the ongoing forensic excavation.
Evidence status
Partially supported, imprecisely stated — human remains were confirmed in an underground chamber structure, which is documented fact. Whether "septic tank" is an accurate description of that specific structure, and the manner and intent behind how remains came to be there, is not settled by the 2017 statement and is an area the ongoing 2025–2027 excavation is expected to clarify further.
Unresolved questions
What was the chamber structure's original function, and was it in active use during the period remains were interred? Who was responsible for placing remains there, and under what circumstances? These are explicitly still open, per ODAIT's own public updates.

"The Bon Secours Sisters deliberately buried children secretly in a disused sewage tank to hide the deaths."

Where this claim has appeared
Widely repeated in media and public commentary since 2017, often stated as settled fact.
Relevant source documents
Fifth Interim Report of the Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation, 15 March 2019 (Document Library, TRP-004); Irish Times report on the same, 5 June 2019.
Exact source wording
"[The structure] was not a recognised burial ground or purpose-built burial chamber"… it "did not provide for the dignified interment of human remains" and was "likely" related to the treatment of sewage and/or waste water. "If it had been designed as a crypt, an entirely different type of structure would have been expected." The Commission also found it "likely the burials were conducted on the instructions of the sisters."

— Fifth Interim Report, Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation, March 2019, as reported by the Irish Times

The other side of this specific finding
The Commission shared its draft technical findings with the Bon Secours congregation before publishing. The congregation commissioned its own expert review, which "suggested that the [Commission's] archaeological reports did not adequately consider the possibility that the structure in which the human remains were found was designed as a burial vault and then used as such" — in other words, the Sisters' own expert disputes the Commission's reading of the physical evidence. It is also on record that Galway County Council — which owned the home and had the legal duty to keep a burial register — did not respond when the Commission shared its draft and technical reports with it in 2017 and 2018.
Plain-English explanation
The Commission's official finding is that the structure was not a proper burial ground and did not provide dignified interment, and that burial there was likely done on the Sisters' instructions. That is the official record. But "deliberately hidden to conceal deaths" is a claim about intent that goes beyond what the Commission's finding states directly, and it is contested by the Sisters' own expert on the physical evidence itself. Separately, no burial register was ever kept by Galway County Council either, despite it being under a legal obligation to keep one — a State and local-authority failure that runs alongside the Sisters' role, not instead of it.
Evidence status
Officially found, but part of it is contested — that burial there was inappropriate and not dignified is an official finding. That it amounted to a deliberate cover-up, specifically to hide deaths, is an interpretation of that finding, not the finding's own wording, and the underlying archaeological reading itself is disputed by an expert engaged by the Bon Secours Sisters.
Unresolved questions
What specific instructions were given, by whom, and why. Why Galway County Council kept no burial register despite the legal duty to do so. Whether the disputed "burial vault" interpretation holds up against the ongoing forensic excavation evidence.

"The extremely high number of child deaths at Tuam shows deliberate cruelty or neglect unique to this home, or to the Bon Secours Sisters."

Where this claim has appeared
Common framing in media coverage and public commentary since 2017.
Relevant source documents
Final Report of the Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation (TRP-001); peer-reviewed study, "Infant Mortality in Mother and Baby Homes in 20th Century Ireland," Population Research and Policy Review, 2024.
What the figures show
Around 3,251 children were born at or admitted to the Tuam institution between 1921 and 1961; the Commission's Final Report records 978 child deaths over that period (roughly 29%). In 1933, the death rate reached 38.95%, about six times the national rate of 6.5% that year, and stayed above 30% intermittently until 1949 before falling. Ireland's national infant mortality rate itself was high by later standards throughout this period — 70 per 1,000 live births in 1930, 65 per 1,000 in 1940.
The 2024 peer-reviewed study, examining several mother and baby homes together, found excess mortality reaching roughly four times the national rate in the 1930s and 1940s across the homes studied — not only at Tuam — and that mortality rates in the homes "mainly resembled mortality rates among all infants of unmarried mothers" more broadly in Irish society at the time. The same study found a similar pattern of very high peak mortality (300–400 deaths per 1,000) at other homes run by different religious orders.
Plain-English explanation
Child mortality at Tuam was severely elevated compared with the general national rate — that part is well documented and not in dispute. But independent academic research comparing several mother and baby homes finds a broadly similar pattern of excess mortality across homes run by different orders, and links a meaningful part of that excess to the wider, well-documented risks facing children of unmarried mothers in Irish society generally during this period — not only to conditions inside any one institution. That context does not reduce the scale of loss, but the comparative evidence does not support singling out Tuam, or the Bon Secours Sisters specifically, as uniquely responsible for a pattern that appears across the wider system.
Evidence status
Documented pattern, not shown to be unique to Tuam or to this order
Unresolved questions
How much of Tuam's specific excess mortality is attributable to conditions inside the home itself (staffing, nutrition, medical care) versus these wider societal factors is a detailed question the Commission's Final Report goes into at length, and deserves its own fuller write-up as we add more of that report to the Document Library.

"There was no mass grave at Tuam, and no evidence that children were buried there."

Where this claim has appeared
In an October 2014 email from Terry Prone of The Communications Clinic, written on behalf of the Sisters of Bon Secours to a French television producer who had contacted the congregation about filming. Prone later confirmed to The Journal that she had sent the email and said there was “absolutely no difference” between its substance and public pronouncements made by the Sisters.
Relevant source documents
Prone email on behalf of Bon Secours, October 2014 (Document Library, TRP-006); Commission of Investigation statement on the Tuam test excavation, 3 March 2017 (TRP-002); Sisters of Bon Secours apology statement, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008).
Exact source wording
"If you come here, you’ll find no mass grave, no evidence that children were ever so buried, and a local police force casting their eyes to heaven and saying “Yeah, a few bones were found – but this was an area where Famine victims were buried. So?”"

— Terry Prone, Communications Clinic, email on behalf of the Sisters of Bon Secours, October 2014, as published by The Journal

"In this second structure, significant quantities of human remains have been discovered in at least 17 of the 20 underground chambers which were examined."

— Commission of Investigation, 3 March 2017 (TRP-002)

"We acknowledge in particular that infants and children who died at the Home were buried in a disrespectful and unacceptable way. For all that, we are deeply sorry."

— Sisters of Bon Secours, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008)

Plain-English explanation
The 2014 statement, made on the congregation’s behalf, asserted that there was no mass grave and no evidence of children buried on the site, and pointed instead to famine-era burials. That position does not survive the later official record: in March 2017 the Commission confirmed significant quantities of human remains in underground chambers on the site, dated to the period the home was operating, and in January 2021 the Sisters themselves apologised for the disrespectful burial of infants and children who died at the home. Whether “mass grave” is the most precise archaeological label remains a separate wording question (see the first entry on this page); the claim that there was no evidence of children buried there is contradicted by subsequent official findings and by the congregation’s own later acknowledgment.
Evidence status
Contradicted by later official findings — that the 2014 denial was made on the Sisters’ behalf is documented. That there was “no evidence” of children buried at the site is not supported by the Commission’s 2017 findings or by the Sisters’ 2021 apology.
Unresolved questions
What information was available to the congregation and its representatives in 2014 about burial practices at Tuam, and on what basis the famine-burial explanation was offered. Those are questions about knowledge and briefing at the time, not about whether remains of children from the home period were later confirmed on the site.

"The Catholic Church / Archdiocese of Tuam ran the Tuam mother and baby home."

Where this claim has appeared
Common shorthand in media coverage and public discussion, often used interchangeably with references to the Bon Secours Sisters or to the Catholic Church more broadly.
Relevant source documents
Statement by Archbishop Michael Neary, 13 January 2021 (Document Library, TRP-009); Final Report of the Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation (TRP-001); Sisters of Bon Secours apology statement, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008).
Exact source wording
"The Tuam Children’s Home was owned by Galway County Council and operated by the Bon Secours Sisters. The Council, on behalf of the State, was responsible for the residents. While the diocese never had an administration role in the running of the Home, it had a pastoral one, in that the priests of Tuam parish served as chaplains."

— Archbishop Michael Neary, Archbishop of Tuam, 13 January 2021 (TRP-009)

"Our Sisters ran St Mary’s Mother and Baby Home in Tuam from 1925 to 1961."

— Sisters of Bon Secours, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008)

Plain-English explanation
Ownership and day-to-day operation were not the same thing. The building and site were owned by Galway County Council; the Bon Secours Sisters — a Catholic religious congregation — operated the home under arrangement with the local authority; the Archdiocese of Tuam says it had no administrative role and that parish priests served as chaplains. Calling the home simply “Church-run” collapses those distinct roles. At the same time, Archbishop Neary’s own statement apologises for the Church’s part in a culture that labelled, judged, stigmatised and ostracised unmarried mothers and their children, so clarifying operational roles is not the same as saying the Church had no responsibility for the wider system in which the home existed.
Evidence status
Imprecise as stated — the home was operated by a Catholic religious order on a local-authority-owned site; the Archdiocese of Tuam states it did not administer the home. Treating “the Catholic Church,” “the Archdiocese,” and “the Bon Secours Sisters” as interchangeable operators does not match the sourced record of ownership and administration.
Unresolved questions
How pastoral chaplaincy interacted with day-to-day institutional decisions, and how far diocesan or congregational oversight reached in practice, remain matters for the detailed institutional history in the Commission’s Final Report rather than for a single shorthand phrase.

"The Bon Secours Sisters never apologised for what happened at Tuam."

Where this claim has appeared
Repeated in public commentary, especially in the years after the 2017 excavation announcement, when contemporaneous reporting noted that the congregation issued no apology at that time (see TRP-007). The claim is sometimes still stated as if it remained true after January 2021.
Relevant source documents
Sisters of Bon Secours statement after the excavation announcement, 3 March 2017 (Document Library, TRP-007); Sisters of Bon Secours apology statement, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008); statement by Archbishop Michael Neary, 13 January 2021 (TRP-009); statement of Archbishop Eamon Martin, 12 January 2021 (TRP-012).
Exact source wording
"The Bon Secours sisters are fully committed to the work of the Commission regarding the mother and baby home in Tuam. On the closing of the Home in 1961 all the records for the Home were returned to Galway County Council who are the owners and occupiers of the lands of the Home. We can therefore make no comment on today’s announcement, other than to confirm our continued cooperation with and support for the work of the Commission in seeking the truth about the home."

— Sisters of Bon Secours, 3 March 2017 (TRP-007)

"We did not live up to our Christianity when running the Home. We failed to respect the inherent dignity of the women and children who came to the Home. We failed to offer them the compassion that they so badly needed. We were part of the system in which they suffered hardship, loneliness and terrible hurt. We acknowledge in particular that infants and children who died at the Home were buried in a disrespectful and unacceptable way. For all that, we are deeply sorry. We offer our profound apologies to all the women and children of St Mary’s Mother and Baby Home, to their families and to the people of this country."

— Sister Eileen O’Connor, Area Leader, Sisters of Bon Secours Ireland, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008)

Plain-English explanation
It is accurate that in March 2017, when human remains were confirmed on the site, the Sisters’ public statement offered cooperation with the Commission but no apology. It is not accurate to say they never apologised. On 13 January 2021, following publication of the Commission’s Final Report, the congregation issued a formal apology that accepted failure to respect the dignity of women and children at the home and specifically acknowledged disrespectful burial of infants and children who died there. Separately, senior Catholic Church leaders — including the Archbishop of Tuam and the Primate of All Ireland — also issued apologies around the same dates (TRP-009, TRP-012). Whether survivors or the public regard those apologies as adequate is a judgment about sufficiency, not about whether an apology was issued.
Evidence status
True for 2017; false if stated as ongoing after January 2021 — no apology in the Sisters’ March 2017 statement is documented; a formal apology in January 2021 is also documented in their own words.
Unresolved questions
What the apology covers in practical terms — including participation in redress and engagement with the ongoing excavation and identification work — continues to develop and should be tracked against later congregational and State statements as they are added to the Document Library.
