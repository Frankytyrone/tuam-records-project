Claim vs Evidence

Each entry below takes a specific public claim and sets it directly against the exact wording of the source documents. The evidence status reflects what the documents actually say — no more, and no less — including where the record is still incomplete. More entries are added as they are researched and checked.

"The children's remains were dumped in a septic tank."

Where this claim has appeared
Widely repeated in national and international media coverage since 2017, and in general public discussion of the Tuam site.
Relevant source documents
Commission of Investigation statement, 3 March 2017 (Document Library, TRP-002); ODAIT forensic updates, 2025–2026 (TRP-010, TRP-011).
Exact source wording
"Test trenches were dug revealing two large structures. One structure appears to be a large sewage containment system or septic tank that had been decommissioned and filled with rubble and debris and then covered with top soil. The second structure is a long structure which is divided into 20 chambers. The Commission has not yet determined what the purpose of this structure was but it appears to be related to the treatment/containment of sewage and/or waste water... In this second structure, significant quantities of human remains have been discovered in at least 17 of the 20 underground chambers which were examined."

— Commission of Investigation, 3 March 2017

Plain-English explanation
The Commission's own 2017 statement describes two separate structures. The first — the one it explicitly calls a decommissioned "sewage containment system or septic tank" — was filled with rubble and covered over; no remains were reported found in it. The remains were found in the second structure, a 20-chambered underground feature whose original purpose the Commission said it had "not yet determined," beyond noting it appeared sewage- or wastewater-related. In short: the popular shorthand "septic tank" conflates the two structures. The precise nature of the chamber where remains were actually found — and how the remains came to be there — is something the Commission itself said was not yet established in 2017, and it remains a subject of the ongoing forensic excavation.
Evidence status
Partially supported, imprecisely stated — human remains were confirmed in an underground chamber structure, which is documented fact. Whether "septic tank" is an accurate description of that specific structure, and the manner and intent behind how remains came to be there, is not settled by the 2017 statement and is an area the ongoing 2025–2027 excavation is expected to clarify further.
Unresolved questions
What was the chamber structure's original function, and was it in active use during the period remains were interred? Who was responsible for placing remains there, and under what circumstances? These are explicitly still open, per ODAIT's own public updates.

"The Bon Secours Sisters deliberately buried children secretly in a disused sewage tank to hide the deaths."

Where this claim has appeared
Widely repeated in media and public commentary since 2017, often stated as settled fact.
Relevant source documents
Fifth Interim Report of the Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation, 15 March 2019 (Document Library, TRP-004); Irish Times report on the same, 5 June 2019.
Exact source wording
"[The structure] was not a recognised burial ground or purpose-built burial chamber"… it "did not provide for the dignified interment of human remains" and was "likely" related to the treatment of sewage and/or waste water. "If it had been designed as a crypt, an entirely different type of structure would have been expected." The Commission also found it "likely the burials were conducted on the instructions of the sisters."

— Fifth Interim Report, Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation, March 2019, as reported by the Irish Times

The other side of this specific finding
The Commission shared its draft technical findings with the Bon Secours congregation before publishing. The congregation commissioned its own expert review, which "suggested that the [Commission's] archaeological reports did not adequately consider the possibility that the structure in which the human remains were found was designed as a burial vault and then used as such" — in other words, the Sisters' own expert disputes the Commission's reading of the physical evidence. It is also on record that Galway County Council — which owned the home and had the legal duty to keep a burial register — did not respond when the Commission shared its draft and technical reports with it in 2017 and 2018.
Plain-English explanation
The Commission's official finding is that the structure was not a proper burial ground and did not provide dignified interment, and that burial there was likely done on the Sisters' instructions. That is the official record. But "deliberately hidden to conceal deaths" is a claim about intent that goes beyond what the Commission's finding states directly, and it is contested by the Sisters' own expert on the physical evidence itself. Separately, no burial register was ever kept by Galway County Council either, despite it being under a legal obligation to keep one — a State and local-authority failure that runs alongside the Sisters' role, not instead of it.
Evidence status
Officially found, but part of it is contested — that burial there was inappropriate and not dignified is an official finding. That it amounted to a deliberate cover-up, specifically to hide deaths, is an interpretation of that finding, not the finding's own wording, and the underlying archaeological reading itself is disputed by an expert engaged by the Bon Secours Sisters.
Unresolved questions
What specific instructions were given, by whom, and why. Why Galway County Council kept no burial register despite the legal duty to do so. Whether the disputed "burial vault" interpretation holds up against the ongoing forensic excavation evidence.

"The extremely high number of child deaths at Tuam shows deliberate cruelty or neglect unique to this home, or to the Bon Secours Sisters."

Where this claim has appeared
Common framing in media coverage and public commentary since 2017.
Relevant source documents
Final Report of the Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation (TRP-001); peer-reviewed study, "Infant Mortality in Mother and Baby Homes in 20th Century Ireland," Population Research and Policy Review, 2024.
What the figures show
Around 3,251 children were born at or admitted to the Tuam institution between 1921 and 1961; the Commission's Final Report records 978 child deaths over that period (roughly 29%). In 1933, the death rate reached 38.95%, about six times the national rate of 6.5% that year, and stayed above 30% intermittently until 1949 before falling. Ireland's national infant mortality rate itself was high by later standards throughout this period — 70 per 1,000 live births in 1930, 65 per 1,000 in 1940.
The 2024 peer-reviewed study, examining several mother and baby homes together, found excess mortality reaching roughly four times the national rate in the 1930s and 1940s across the homes studied — not only at Tuam — and that mortality rates in the homes "mainly resembled mortality rates among all infants of unmarried mothers" more broadly in Irish society at the time. The same study found a similar pattern of very high peak mortality (300–400 deaths per 1,000) at other homes run by different religious orders.
Plain-English explanation
Child mortality at Tuam was severely elevated compared with the general national rate — that part is well documented and not in dispute. But independent academic research comparing several mother and baby homes finds a broadly similar pattern of excess mortality across homes run by different orders, and links a meaningful part of that excess to the wider, well-documented risks facing children of unmarried mothers in Irish society generally during this period — not only to conditions inside any one institution. That context does not reduce the scale of loss, but the comparative evidence does not support singling out Tuam, or the Bon Secours Sisters specifically, as uniquely responsible for a pattern that appears across the wider system.
Evidence status
Documented pattern, not shown to be unique to Tuam or to this order
Unresolved questions
How much of Tuam's specific excess mortality is attributable to conditions inside the home itself (staffing, nutrition, medical care) versus these wider societal factors is a detailed question the Commission's Final Report goes into at length, and deserves its own fuller write-up as we add more of that report to the Document Library.

"There was no mass grave at Tuam, and no evidence that children were buried there."

Where this claim has appeared
In an October 2014 email from Terry Prone of The Communications Clinic, written on behalf of the Sisters of Bon Secours to a French television producer who had contacted the congregation about filming. Prone later confirmed to The Journal that she had sent the email and said there was “absolutely no difference” between its substance and public pronouncements made by the Sisters.
Relevant source documents
Prone email on behalf of Bon Secours, October 2014 (Document Library, TRP-006); Commission of Investigation statement on the Tuam test excavation, 3 March 2017 (TRP-002); Sisters of Bon Secours apology statement, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008).
Exact source wording
"If you come here, you’ll find no mass grave, no evidence that children were ever so buried, and a local police force casting their eyes to heaven and saying “Yeah, a few bones were found – but this was an area where Famine victims were buried. So?”"

— Terry Prone, Communications Clinic, email on behalf of the Sisters of Bon Secours, October 2014, as published by The Journal

"In this second structure, significant quantities of human remains have been discovered in at least 17 of the 20 underground chambers which were examined."

— Commission of Investigation, 3 March 2017 (TRP-002)

"We acknowledge in particular that infants and children who died at the Home were buried in a disrespectful and unacceptable way. For all that, we are deeply sorry."

— Sisters of Bon Secours, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008)

Plain-English explanation
The 2014 statement, made on the congregation’s behalf, asserted that there was no mass grave and no evidence of children buried on the site, and pointed instead to famine-era burials. That position does not survive the later official record: in March 2017 the Commission confirmed significant quantities of human remains in underground chambers on the site, dated to the period the home was operating, and in January 2021 the Sisters themselves apologised for the disrespectful burial of infants and children who died at the home. Whether “mass grave” is the most precise archaeological label remains a separate wording question (see the first entry on this page); the claim that there was no evidence of children buried there is contradicted by subsequent official findings and by the congregation’s own later acknowledgment.
Evidence status
Contradicted by later official findings — that the 2014 denial was made on the Sisters’ behalf is documented. That there was “no evidence” of children buried at the site is not supported by the Commission’s 2017 findings or by the Sisters’ 2021 apology.
Unresolved questions
What information was available to the congregation and its representatives in 2014 about burial practices at Tuam, and on what basis the famine-burial explanation was offered. Those are questions about knowledge and briefing at the time, not about whether remains of children from the home period were later confirmed on the site.

"The Catholic Church / Archdiocese of Tuam ran the Tuam mother and baby home."

Where this claim has appeared
Common shorthand in media coverage and public discussion, often used interchangeably with references to the Bon Secours Sisters or to the Catholic Church more broadly.
Relevant source documents
Statement by Archbishop Michael Neary, 13 January 2021 (Document Library, TRP-009); Final Report of the Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation (TRP-001); Sisters of Bon Secours apology statement, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008).
Exact source wording
"The Tuam Children’s Home was owned by Galway County Council and operated by the Bon Secours Sisters. The Council, on behalf of the State, was responsible for the residents. While the diocese never had an administration role in the running of the Home, it had a pastoral one, in that the priests of Tuam parish served as chaplains."

— Archbishop Michael Neary, Archbishop of Tuam, 13 January 2021 (TRP-009)

"Our Sisters ran St Mary’s Mother and Baby Home in Tuam from 1925 to 1961."

— Sisters of Bon Secours, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008)

Plain-English explanation
Ownership and day-to-day operation were not the same thing. The building and site were owned by Galway County Council; the Bon Secours Sisters — a Catholic religious congregation — operated the home under arrangement with the local authority; the Archdiocese of Tuam says it had no administrative role and that parish priests served as chaplains. Calling the home simply “Church-run” collapses those distinct roles. At the same time, Archbishop Neary’s own statement apologises for the Church’s part in a culture that labelled, judged, stigmatised and ostracised unmarried mothers and their children, so clarifying operational roles is not the same as saying the Church had no responsibility for the wider system in which the home existed.
Evidence status
Imprecise as stated — the home was operated by a Catholic religious order on a local-authority-owned site; the Archdiocese of Tuam states it did not administer the home. Treating “the Catholic Church,” “the Archdiocese,” and “the Bon Secours Sisters” as interchangeable operators does not match the sourced record of ownership and administration.
Unresolved questions
How pastoral chaplaincy interacted with day-to-day institutional decisions, and how far diocesan or congregational oversight reached in practice, remain matters for the detailed institutional history in the Commission’s Final Report rather than for a single shorthand phrase.

"The Bon Secours Sisters never apologised for what happened at Tuam."

Where this claim has appeared
Repeated in public commentary, especially in the years after the 2017 excavation announcement, when contemporaneous reporting noted that the congregation issued no apology at that time (see TRP-007). The claim is sometimes still stated as if it remained true after January 2021.
Relevant source documents
Sisters of Bon Secours statement after the excavation announcement, 3 March 2017 (Document Library, TRP-007); Sisters of Bon Secours apology statement, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008); statement by Archbishop Michael Neary, 13 January 2021 (TRP-009); statement of Archbishop Eamon Martin, 12 January 2021 (TRP-012).
Exact source wording
"The Bon Secours sisters are fully committed to the work of the Commission regarding the mother and baby home in Tuam. On the closing of the Home in 1961 all the records for the Home were returned to Galway County Council who are the owners and occupiers of the lands of the Home. We can therefore make no comment on today’s announcement, other than to confirm our continued cooperation with and support for the work of the Commission in seeking the truth about the home."

— Sisters of Bon Secours, 3 March 2017 (TRP-007)

"We did not live up to our Christianity when running the Home. We failed to respect the inherent dignity of the women and children who came to the Home. We failed to offer them the compassion that they so badly needed. We were part of the system in which they suffered hardship, loneliness and terrible hurt. We acknowledge in particular that infants and children who died at the Home were buried in a disrespectful and unacceptable way. For all that, we are deeply sorry. We offer our profound apologies to all the women and children of St Mary’s Mother and Baby Home, to their families and to the people of this country."

— Sister Eileen O’Connor, Area Leader, Sisters of Bon Secours Ireland, 13 January 2021 (TRP-008)

Plain-English explanation
It is accurate that in March 2017, when human remains were confirmed on the site, the Sisters’ public statement offered cooperation with the Commission but no apology. It is not accurate to say they never apologised. On 13 January 2021, following publication of the Commission’s Final Report, the congregation issued a formal apology that accepted failure to respect the dignity of women and children at the home and specifically acknowledged disrespectful burial of infants and children who died there. Separately, senior Catholic Church leaders — including the Archbishop of Tuam and the Primate of All Ireland — also issued apologies around the same dates (TRP-009, TRP-012). Whether survivors or the public regard those apologies as adequate is a judgment about sufficiency, not about whether an apology was issued.
Evidence status
True for 2017; false if stated as ongoing after January 2021 — no apology in the Sisters’ March 2017 statement is documented; a formal apology in January 2021 is also documented in their own words.
Unresolved questions
What the apology covers in practical terms — including participation in redress and engagement with the ongoing excavation and identification work — continues to develop and should be tracked against later congregational and State statements as they are added to the Document Library.

"Nearly 800 babies' skeletons were found dumped in a septic tank" — reported as an established fact by international media in June 2014.

Where this claim has appeared
Headlines in June 2014 across major outlets, including The Washington Post ("Bodies of 800 babies, long-dead, found in septic tank"), Al Jazeera ("Nearly 800 dead babies found in septic tank in Ireland"), the New York Daily News ("800 skeletons of babies found inside tank"), the Boston Globe ("Galway historian finds 800 babies in septic tank grave"), Buzzfeed, ABC News Australia, and an opinion piece in The Guardian originally headlined "Tell us the truth about the children dumped in Galway's mass graves." This reporting predated any excavation of the site — the first test excavation did not take place until November 2016–February 2017.
Relevant source documents
Connacht Tribune report on Corless's campaign, 13 February 2014 (Document Library, TRP-021); Irish Times interview with Catherine Corless, 7 June 2014 (TRP-013); The Guardian's own correction note on its 4 June 2014 piece (TRP-022); Associated Press formal correction, 20 June 2014 (TRP-014); Commission of Investigation statement on the 2017 test excavation (TRP-002).
Exact source wording
"I thought why would there be a crypt in the middle of nowhere. I went looking at old maps and found there was a septic tank marked on a 1891 map belonging to the home. The tank became defunct in 1938… It appears they made a crypt out of the old septic tank. I'd hope they'd have at least cleaned it out."

— Catherine Corless, quoted in the Connacht Tribune, 13 February 2014, more than three months before the story went international (TRP-021)

"I never used that word 'dumped'... I never said to anyone that 800 bodies were dumped in a septic tank. That did not come from me at any point. They are not my words."

— Catherine Corless, quoted in the Irish Times, 7 June 2014 (TRP-013)

"The headline and introduction of this article were amended on 10 June 2014 to better reflect the fact that the children's remains are believed to have been buried in a disused sewage tank. This fact has yet to be proved, as do the facts behind how any such mass burial may have come [about]."

— The Guardian, editor's correction note appended to its own 4 June 2014 piece (TRP-022)

"In the June 3 story, the AP quoted a researcher who said she believed that most of the remains of children who died there were interred in a disused septic tank; the researcher has since clarified that without excavation and forensic analysis it is impossible to know how many sets of remains the tank contains, if any."

— Associated Press correction, 20 June 2014 (TRP-014)

Plain-English explanation
Corless's own earliest on-record framing — given to her local paper in February 2014, months before this became an international story — was that the nuns had "made a crypt out of the old septic tank," a considered hypothesis based on comparing an 1890s Ordnance Survey map with a 1970s account of two local boys finding skeletons on the site. It was not a claim that 800 bodies had been confirmed "dumped" there. When the story went global in late May/June 2014, headlines across multiple major outlets dropped the hedge words and reported flatly that around 800 skeletons had already been found, dumped, in a septic tank — nearly three years before any excavation occurred. Two of the outlets involved went on to correct themselves in the same month: the Associated Press issued a formal correction, and The Guardian itself appended an editor's note to its own opinion piece just six days after publishing it, acknowledging the burial claim was unproven. Corless was on record around the same time explicitly rejecting the word "dumped" as her own. This does not mean nothing serious happened at Tuam — the 2017 test excavation did confirm significant human remains on the site (see the entry above on the two structures) — but the specific claim that a confirmed count of ~800 bodies had been found, dumped, in a septic tank, as of mid-2014, was a documented exaggeration of Corless's own more cautious, unexcavated hypothesis, corrected within weeks by more than one of the outlets that had first reported it.
Evidence status
Media exaggeration, documented and corrected by more than one outlet — that this was reported as settled fact in June 2014 is documented; that it accurately reflected either Corless's own claims or the state of the evidence at the time is not supported, and is contradicted by Corless's own on-record statements (in February and again in June 2014) and by both the Associated Press's and The Guardian's own published corrections.
Unresolved questions
How many of the many outlets that ran the "800 dumped" framing in 2014 issued their own corrections (the Associated Press and The Guardian are the clearest documented cases found so far for this site; whether others did the same has not been fully checked). What the true total and distribution of remains at the site is remains a question for the ongoing 2025–2027 forensic excavation, not for 2014-era press reports.

"Babies who died at the home were denied Catholic baptism and a Christian burial, because Church teaching required this for the children of unmarried mothers."

Where this claim has appeared
Reported as fact in international coverage of the Tuam story in June 2014, including in Associated Press reports of 3 and 8 June 2014.
Relevant source documents
Associated Press formal correction, 20 June 2014 (Document Library, TRP-014).
Exact source wording
"The Associated Press incorrectly reported that the children had not received Roman Catholic baptisms; documents show that many children at the orphanage were baptized. The AP also incorrectly reported that Catholic teaching at the time was to deny baptism and Christian burial to the children of unwed mothers; although that may have occurred in practice at times it was not church teaching."

— Associated Press correction, 20 June 2014 (TRP-014)

Plain-English explanation
Two distinct things were reported as fact and both required correction. First, that the children who died at Tuam had not been baptised at all — contradicted by a Tuam Archdiocese registry showing the home had baptised more than 2,000 children. Second, that denying baptism to the children of unmarried mothers was official Catholic Church teaching — the AP's own correction states plainly that it was not, while allowing that individual denial "may have occurred in practice at times," which is a different and narrower claim than a doctrinal policy.
Evidence status
Contradicted by parish records; retracted by the reporting outlet — both the blanket "never baptised" claim and the "Church doctrine required it" claim are documented as incorrect and formally corrected.
Unresolved questions
Whether baptism or Christian burial was ever refused to a specific child at Tuam in individual practice — as distinct from a mistaken claim about official doctrine — is not addressed by the AP's correction and has not yet been checked against a primary source for this site.

"The 20-chamber structure is not a burial site from the Home's operational period at all — it is an 'ossuary' that Galway County Council built in the late 1970s or early 1980s to re-inter remains disturbed by building an access road and a playground, and disarticulated bones and a post-1968 artefact prove it."

Where this claim has appeared
Brian Nugent, an independent researcher and self-published author, in his self-published book @tuambabies: A critical look at the Tuam Children's Home scandal (Lulu.com, first published 2015); repeated in a broadcast interview carried by Gript Media on 30 July 2019, and in public talks, including one in Tuam in 2019 reported by the Avondhu Newspaper.
Relevant source documents
Brian Nugent, @tuambabies and 2019 broadcast interview (Document Library, TRP-016, TRP-017); Fifth Interim Report of the Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation, 15 March 2019, paragraphs 8.109–8.139 (TRP-004); Avondhu Newspaper account of Nugent's 2019 Tuam talk.
Exact source wording
"[This is] a structure deliberately created by the county council to house the re-interred remains… in my opinion, late 1970s, early 1980s… a structure built for the re-internment of remains that were elsewhere in the graveyard."

— Brian Nugent, broadcast interview, 30 July 2019 (TRP-017)

"It seems clear that relatively extensive work and construction was conducted in and around the site of the Children's Home in Tuam, particularly during the July – December 1937 period. The Commission thinks it possible that the reworking of the old sewage tank and the construction of the second structure described above may have occurred at this time."

— Fifth Interim Report, Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation, para. 8.109 (TRP-004)

"The soil scientist confirmed that the findings of the Osteoarchaeologist in relation to articulation corroborated the findings that human remains decomposed in situ. The Commission does not consider that there is any real doubt that a considerable number of the children in the tanks decomposed in this way."

— Fifth Interim Report, para. 8.130 (TRP-004)

"[A bottle marked 'Castrol GTX'…] this product was released into the UK market for the first time on 18 April 1968… This information proves that this particular chamber at least was accessible either temporarily or for an extended period of time post 1968."

— Fifth Interim Report, para. 8.137 (TRP-004)

Plain-English explanation
Nugent's theory rests on three planks: a later construction date for the structure (1970s–80s, after the Home closed in 1961), disarticulated ("jumbled") bones as evidence that remains were dug up elsewhere and moved in, and a Castrol GTX oil-can artefact he says proves a post-1968 date. The Commission's own Fifth Interim Report addresses all three directly, using much of the same category of underlying evidence, and reaches different conclusions. On dating, it points to documented construction work at the Home specifically in mid-to-late 1937 and considers it possible the structure was built then — not in the 1970s or 1980s. On the bones, its own osteoarchaeologist and soil scientist examined the chambers for "articulation" (whether bones remained in the position expected if a body decomposed where it lay) and found evidence of it in multiple chambers, concluding remains had "decomposed in situ" rather than being moved there after death. On the artefact, the Commission does confirm the same Castrol GTX oil can Nugent cites, and dates it the same way (available in Ireland from April 1968) — but draws the narrower conclusion that this particular chamber was accessed or disturbed at some point after 1968, not that the entire structure was newly built at that time. Separately, according to a contemporaneous account of a 2019 public talk Nugent gave in Tuam, Catherine Corless asked him directly whether he had brought this theory to the Commission of Investigation for examination, and he said he had not.
Evidence status
Contradicted by the official technical findings — that a Castrol GTX artefact dated to after 1968 was found in one chamber is confirmed in the Commission's own report, and is a genuine documented fact. That the whole structure was built decades after the Home closed, or that its contents show disarticulation rather than in-situ decomposition, is contradicted by the same report's own dating analysis (pointing to 1937) and by its osteoarchaeological and soil-science findings.
Unresolved questions
The Commission's own report does not claim to have fully resolved everything: it states plainly that it has not established what the chamber structure's original purpose was, nor whether it was ever actually used for sewage or waste-water treatment, and it explicitly raises — without answering — where children who died at the Home before 1937 might be buried. Those remain genuinely open questions in the official record itself, separate from, and not resolved by, Nugent's re-interment theory.

"None of the children who died at the Tuam home were given a proper burial — they were simply dumped, without coffins or any dignity, wherever their remains were found."

Where this claim has appeared
Implicit in much general commentary since 2014 describing all burials at the site as an undifferentiated "dumping." Explicitly challenged in a Catholic Herald column by David Quinn, 17 April 2026.
Relevant source documents
ODAIT Technical Update 6, reporting to 31 March 2026 (Document Library, TRP-018); ODAIT Technical Update 8, 22 July 2026 (TRP-015); David Quinn, "What really happened at Tuam?", The Catholic Herald, 17 April 2026 (TRP-019).
Exact source wording
"Results of excavation indicate that these infants were buried in coffins which have since decayed. The evidence indicates that the majority of coffins were single shouldered with mounts and had been painted white. In a small number of instances, two or three individuals were buried in the same coffin."

— ODAIT, Technical Update 6 (TRP-018)

"[ODAIT] uncovered a pit (over 3 metres in width)… It contains modern building debris consistent with the post-institutional era… An examination of available aerial photography indicates that this disturbance took place sometime after 1977."

— ODAIT, Technical Update 5/6 (TRP-018)

Plain-English explanation
There are two distinct locations at the Tuam site, and the evidence differs for each. The 20-chamber structure examined in 2016–17 (see the first entry on this page) remains only partially understood, and its excavation has not resumed as of the most recent update. Separately, in a different part of the site identified in historical planning documents as a "burial ground," ODAIT's 2025–26 excavation has so far recovered 99 infants, and every one of them, in both areas excavated to date, was found buried in a proper coffin — most commonly a single-shouldered, white-painted design, consistent with a genuine, organised burial practice rather than an undifferentiated dumping. ODAIT has also confirmed that part of this same burial ground was later disturbed — a wide pit, dug with a mechanical digger sometime after 1977 (during the construction of the housing estate now on the site) and backfilled with rubble, which appears to have damaged some existing burials. That later disturbance is a genuinely documented fact, separate from, and not the same claim as, Brian Nugent's specific theory (addressed in the entry above) that the 20-chamber structure itself was newly built during that period.
Evidence status
Contradicted, for the burial-ground area excavated so far — every infant recovered from Tent 1 and Tent 2 to date was coffined, which is inconsistent with a blanket "no dignity, all dumped" description of burial practice at the site. The separate, still only partly excavated chamber structure is a distinct question, addressed in the earlier entry on this page, and this finding does not resolve it.
Unresolved questions
How the coffined burial ground relates to the chamber structure; how many, if any, of the recovered coffined remains were disturbed by the post-1977 digging and whether all disturbed remains have now been accounted for; and whether coffin burial was a consistent practice across the Home's full 1925–1961 operation or changed over time. These are for the ongoing excavation to determine.

"The nuns forced women to enter the home against their will, and forced or profited from the adoption of the children who survived."

Where this claim has appeared
Common in media coverage and public commentary since 2014 and in campaigns for redress; challenged as inaccurate in Brian Nugent's self-published commentary (Document Library, TRP-016, TRP-020) and in Catholic press commentary.
Relevant source documents
Final Report of the Mother and Baby Homes Commission of Investigation (Document Library, TRP-001), Executive Summary paragraphs 8–9, and main report paragraphs 27–31 and 34–36.
Exact source wording
"There is no evidence that women were forced to enter mother and baby homes by the church or State authorities. Most women had no alternative… Women were brought to mother and baby homes by their parents or other family members without being consulted as to their destination."

— Final Report, Executive Summary, para. 8 (TRP-001)

"They were not 'incarcerated' in the strict meaning of the word but, in the earlier years at least, with some justification, they thought they were. They were always free to leave if they took their child; some did leave before the child was born and some left without their child. Most had no money and nowhere to go."

— Final Report, para. 27 (TRP-001)

"Some former residents and lobby groups have suggested that 'adoption' should be renamed 'forced adoption'. The Commission does not agree. The Commission found very little evidence that children were forcibly taken from their mothers; it accepts that the mothers did not have much choice but that is not the same as 'forced' adoption."

— Final Report, para. 34 (TRP-001)

"The Commission has not seen any evidence that the religious orders who ran the mother and baby homes made a profit from so doing. At various times, it is clear that they struggled to make ends meet and their members were not always paid for their work."

— Final Report, para. 36 (TRP-001)

Plain-English explanation
On the specific, narrow questions of legal coercion and financial profit, the Commission's own findings do not support this claim: it found no evidence that church or State authorities forced women into the homes, no evidence of profiteering by the religious orders that ran them, and very little evidence that children were forcibly removed from their mothers in the sense the term "forced adoption" usually implies. But the same report gives a more complicated picture alongside those findings, not instead of them: women were routinely brought to the homes by their own families "without being consulted," most "had no alternative" in any realistic sense given the poverty and stigma of the time, and the Commission itself notes it could be argued that women who had to stay for years due to unmade alternative arrangements "could be regarded as 'incarcerated'." So "forced by the Church or State" specifically is not supported by the Commission's findings; but "had a real, free choice" would also overstate what the same report says. The Commission's own distinction is that mothers "did not have much choice" without that amounting to legal "force."
Evidence status
Not supported as stated, but the wider picture is more constrained than "free choice" would suggest — the specific claims of institutional coercion and financial profit are contradicted by the Commission's own findings. The broader claim that women had little real alternative, and that some children's placement for adoption happened without their mothers being meaningfully consulted, is separately documented in the same report and is not in dispute.
Unresolved questions
Illegal adoption allegations specifically (as opposed to the general "forced adoption" framing addressed here) are a distinct, ongoing area of research for this site (see the Document Library) and are not resolved by the Commission's general findings quoted above.
